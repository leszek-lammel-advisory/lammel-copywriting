# lammel-copywriting

A complete method for researching and writing crisp, personalized B2B emails with a useful asset CTA. Apply the six-rule writing contract below in full. The same method is available as [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) and [plain text](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leszek-lammel-advisory/lammel-copywriting/main/SKILL.md).

## Use it

```text
Use https://github.com/leszek-lammel-advisory/lammel-copywriting.
Read and apply its complete writing contract and research method.
Seller: [NAME + WEBSITE + EXACT PRODUCT/USE CASE]
Recipient: [NAME + ROLE + COMPANY + WEBSITE]
Research: [OPTIONAL]
```

Web search and page-reading access are needed for missing evidence. All teaching examples are fictional. The complete method follows, so no second file fetch is required.

## Complete method

Write something this particular recipient would want to receive. The operator gives you the seller, exact product, recipient and any available research. You research the missing evidence, choose the angle and design the asset. Never ask the operator to invent the campaign for you.

## The writing contract

Preserve all six rules when reading or summarizing this method:

1. **Start exactly `Hi [first name],` then a blank line, then `Saw `.** The Saw sentence is one supported observation, at most 18 words. Describe one action or change. Never recite a title, remit, responsibilities, systems list or company statistics. Adding Saw to a job description does not make it personalization.
2. **Keep `We help [relevant type of company] [specific outcome].`** Follow it with one verified customer result about that same job. No product feature list and no generic “streamline workflows.” Research proof when the input does not contain it.
3. **Offer one concrete asset.** Ask `Worth sending over [asset]?` Name what it actually shows, such as one sample conversation or a comparison of two named choices. “A tailored blueprint covering X, Y and Z” fails. “Filled-in,” “one-page” and the prospect's company name cannot make a generic offer useful.
4. **Under 80 words, including greeting and any supplied signature.** No em dashes (U+2014), en dashes (U+2013) or double hyphens in subject or body. Put any supplied sender name on its own line without a dash. Never invent a sender.
5. **Keep the exact product and customer job fixed.** Do not use a neighboring product's signal or case study. A shared word such as “payments,” “AI” or “automation” does not establish a match. If research cannot establish a supported angle for the specified product, return an empty email and explain `no_supported_angle` separately.
6. **Check the finished text against these rules and rewrite failures.** Explaining a violation in the reasoning does not make the email acceptable. A disclaimer cannot rescue the wrong product or an unsupported claim.

```text
Hi {{first_name}},

Saw {{one_specific_observation}}.

We help {{relevant_type_of_company}} {{specific_outcome}}. {{customer}} used {{product}} to {{verified_result}}.

Worth sending over {{specific_useful_asset}}?
```

For another requested language, use the natural equivalent of the same structure. Otherwise use the literal English opening above. Follow an explicit user override.

## 1. Find the right work

Read supplied research first. Establish who uses the exact product, what they do with it and who benefits. Compare that with the prospect's actual work before selecting a signal. Verify seller capabilities on its official site when not supplied.

**Example of a false match:** A platform embedding checkout so its customers can collect money is doing a different job from the platform collecting its own software subscription fees. Its accountant reconciling subscription invoices is not evidence of a customer-payments project. A marketplace customer story does not bridge that gap. Do not rescue the mismatch by calling the offer “exploratory.” Find relevant work or return `no_supported_angle`.

Look for **project, owner, change**: what relevant work is happening, who at this company owns it and what makes it current. Prefer an explicit evaluation, migration, rollout or improvement mandate. A job describing a specific change can be useful. Routine responsibilities, tool requirements, funding, headcount and promotions alone are weak. A new leader is supporting context; use an observable task if it gives you a better asset.

Search the work, not just the company name. Replace the brackets:

```text
site:[company-domain] "[workflow phrase]"
"[company name]" "[relevant role family]" jobs
"[company name]" "[change verb]" "[workflow phrase]"
site:linkedin.com/posts "[person name]" "[workflow phrase]"
site:[seller-domain] "[product]" "[workflow]" "customer"
```

Start with official product, careers and announcement pages. Follow the employer's careers link to its hiring platform. Search Google or the available web search; open promising sources rather than relying on snippets. Use `site:`, exact phrases and date filters where supported. If sparse, loosen one constraint or switch source type. Do not keep repeating the same query.

Verify employer, current role, event date and active status. Start with 90 days for events and six months for appointments if no window is supplied; ongoing projects can be older. A repost date is not a new initiative. Former-employer work and a staffing agency's client vacancy do not belong to the target company. Keep facts separate from inferences. Existing product usage calls for an improvement or expansion angle.

Choose one fact that changes the substance of your offer. Growth does not prove a broken process; hiring does not prove overload. A relevant public workflow can support a useful example without claiming a buying window. If no specific initiative is found, inspect that workflow before falling back to generic role-based copy.

## 2. Find proof and design the asset

Search the seller's official customer stories and read the best candidate. Keep the exact product, customer job, result and conditions. A famous logo is not enough. Qualitative proof is valid; metrics, “recently” and timeframes appear only when verified. Never reuse fictional examples as evidence.

Privately finish this connection:

> They are doing X. We help this kind of company achieve Y. Customer Z achieved a comparable result using this product. Asset A shows a useful part of that approach applied to X.

Every part must concern the same task. If the proof or asset concerns a different task, change the angle. Do not omit the commercial middle to make room for more personalization.

Before naming the asset, decide the first useful thing inside it. Could the reader finish “I could use that to…” without guessing? For a comparison, name the two real choices. For a worked example, name the situation it demonstrates. For an annotated walkthrough, identify the public step you can inspect. Then describe that useful content in ordinary language.

The observation must change the asset itself, not just its label. A leadership appointment followed by a generic industry checklist is weak even when both sentences are factual. Research a better work detail or disclose `limited_personalization`.

Offer something feasible from public information or sample inputs. A permission reply should be enough to receive it. Do not promise a private customer playbook, an audit of unseen systems, a working integration or unsupported savings. Unless actually made, offer to prepare or send it; never say “I built” or “I found three issues.” Do not create the asset or send the email as part of this task.

If focused proof research genuinely fails, keep the supported outcome and asset, omit the unsupported customer sentence and disclose `missing_customer_proof` outside the email. Name what you searched or read. Missing proof in the input is not a failed search. If proof is mandatory, mark the requirement unmet. If browsing is unavailable, say so and use only supplied evidence.

## 3. Write like a person

Use research to understand the task, then put the notes away and write one observation. Never paste a person's profile or a job's responsibilities into the opener. Avoid jargon chains and generic benefit claims. Use one concrete verb and one recognizable task.

All examples below are fictional and cannot be used as real evidence.

**Given an explicit mandate to automate invoice matching:**

- Bad: “Your finance remit includes billing, collections and reconciliation.”
- Still bad: “Saw your Finance Operations role calls out billing, collections and bank reconciliation.”
- Good: “Saw Aster is hiring someone to automate invoice matching.”

Do not merely shorten the bad sentence. Pick the one supported action. Do not call routine invoice matching an automation project unless the source actually says so.

**For the asset:**

- Bad: “A personalized payment-ops blueprint covering controls, exceptions and reporting.”
- Still bad: “A filled-in finance workflow map for Aster.”
- Good, if relevant to the product: “An example showing how one payment matches its invoice and bank entry.”

The good version lets the recipient picture the content. An asset should earn its place through usefulness, not impressive terminology.

## Complete fictional example

**Inputs:** Maya leads finance at Aster Studio. Its current vacancy explicitly calls for replacing emailed receipts before opening Warsaw and Berlin offices. ExpenseLane supports cross-border reimbursement approvals. A fictional official customer story says North Studio cut reimbursement time from 12 days to four within six weeks using ExpenseLane and describes its approval flow. The sender can prepare an illustrative example for Aster; no asset exists yet.

> Subject: faster reimbursements
>
> Hi Maya,
>
> Saw you're replacing emailed receipts before opening Warsaw and Berlin.
>
> We help design studios reimburse cross-border expenses faster. With ExpenseLane, North Studio cut reimbursement time from 12 days to four in six weeks.
>
> Worth sending an example of that approval flow for your two new offices?

The specific change determines the example. The outcome and proof concern that same work. It promises no equivalent saving and no private customer material. Without evidence that Maya owns the change, name Aster instead of saying “you're.”

## Final check and output

Read only the proposed email. Check the literal Saw opening, one observation in at most 18 words, explicit We help outcome, relevant verified proof, a visible use for the asset, fewer than 80 total words and no forbidden dashes. Reject any list of duties disguised as an opener. Reject any list of topics disguised as an asset. Verify the exact product connection again. Rewrite before output.

Return the caller's requested fields. Default: `subject`, `body`, `sources`, `note`. Sources identify the actual supporting URLs for signal, capability and proof; label supplied evidence honestly. Keep sources and limitations outside the prospect-facing email. Use the caller's `reasoning` field for these if that is its format. Return empty subject/body fields for `no_supported_angle` or missing essential identity. Never fabricate a URL or claim research you did not perform.
